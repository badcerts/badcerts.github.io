Vitreos

AI watermarking, attribution and provenance

Research, August-October 2026

What can a mark actually tell us?

A convincing document can have a fabricated origin. A genuine document can be falsely accused of being generated. And a person can spend weeks on a piece of work, use a model for one small edit, and discover that a detector's label has swallowed the distinction.

Vitreos investigates the evidence behind those labels. It began with inspections of model outputs and exported files, then moved to controlled local generation, repetition tests and a small RISC-V computer. The question throughout is practical: what can we measure about a document's origin, and how far does that evidence go?

There is a useful result here. A locally generated marked passage retained a key-related signal after repeated token windows were counted only once. There are equally useful negatives: changing token IDs alone did not change decoded text distributions, several searches could not resolve their original question, and some apparent anomalies came from the inspection tools.

This is a case study with reproducible calculations, not a universal detector. It offers a way to examine provenance claims, challenge unsupported accusations, and design better tests without surrendering the evidence to an opaque score.

Four technologies, four different questions

A statistical text watermark is introduced while a model generates text. A key influences choices among possible next tokens. A compatible detector later tests for the resulting relationship. The evidence lives in the sequence of choices; it need not involve invisible characters or a hidden field in a file.

An AI-text classifier estimates whether writing resembles examples from its training data. It can operate without a watermark key. Its answer depends on its training, test population and threshold; polished grammar or a familiar phrase is not a cryptographic signature.

File metadata can record software, dates and editing information. Ordinary metadata may be changed or lost. A text passage copied out of a PDF does not carry every property of the original container with it.

Signed content credentials, such as C2PA, bind provenance assertions to an asset and a signing identity. Validation checks that binding and the relevant trust conditions. It does not decide whether the depicted event happened or whether the author's account is honest. See the technical explanation.

These approaches can complement one another. Confusing them makes a weak test look decisive: finding no unusual Unicode says little about keyed token selection, and finding no embedded credential says little about text that was copied through an export path that did not carry one.

What the providers disclose

As of 11 October 2026, Anthropic describes a SynthID-Text variant; Google publishes a research implementation of SynthID Text; OpenAI describes textGrain and a limited initial rollout. Their keys, implementations and access conditions are not interchangeable. The provider comparison links the dated primary sources and explains the mechanisms.

Vitreos did not recover those providers' keys or defeat their production systems. Its local greenlist experiment tests a specified method under conditions the research could inspect. Earlier provider-output observations remain separate.

The experiments and what they found

A local watermark survived the repetition check

A 1.5-billion-parameter DeepSeek model produced one unmarked and one marked completion from the same prompt and sampling seed. The marked run used a keyed greenlist from the project's experimental software, Axis Provenance Lab (AxisPL): a context-dependent subset of possible tokens received a sampling bias. Each completion reached 256 tokens.

In the supplied key-holder scoring, the marked text had 168 hits among 252 eligible positions. But the passage repeated itself. Counting each exact four-token context plus its following token only once left 93 windows and 60 hits. The clean completion had 53 hits among 251 distinct windows.

The excess did not disappear when exact repetitions were removed. That is a specific result about this pair. It does not supply a calibrated error rate, measure writing quality, or establish that a private provider used this method. The public files let anyone check the token counts and score arithmetic; the original secret-key memberships are supplied evidence. Methods and all scores; record R18.

Repetition can make a statistic look much stronger

The next test used synthetic sequences and 4,096 fixed public test keys. A short motif repeated many times could cross a nominal score threshold even though it contained little new structure. For the 16-window motif repeated 64 times, 1,502 key trials crossed nominal z = 4; one did when exact duplicate windows were counted once. The distinct-window fixture had no raw crossings.

These are results for constructed sequences, not a false-positive rate for human writing. They demonstrate why counting positions as independent evidence can fail. Deduplication repairs that particular source of inflation, but does not automatically make all remaining observations independent. OpenAI's textGrain technical report gives its own detector the same rule: score only the first occurrence of each distinct context window. Control and reproducible code.

A negative search only answers the question it could detect

A retained audit summary covers 17 Markdown files. It found no hits for the specified anomalous character classes. Two lines contained ordinary trailing spaces. That result does not exclude a watermark expressed through otherwise ordinary token choices or separator positions. The original files are not in the public package, so the audit itself cannot be rerun from these summaries.

Comparisons of nine prose pairs and 32 constrained completion pairs found no exact pair matches. A separate scan using 64 arbitrary keys and a surrogate tokenization did not cross its nominal threshold. Without the provider's actual profile, that scan cannot exclude a provider watermark. It is a record of what that particular detector failed to see. Early observations.

The container and the instrument can mislead

Five image/export fixtures lacked the inspected embedded credential structures. Separately, all five inspected HTML segments and all nine PDF segments retained their source word sequences. Converting one passage into several files did not create several independent generations.

Two apparent signals were inspection errors: a raw PDF byte search mistook a substring inside compressed data for an action entry, and one extractor turned ten bullets into a control character. Parsing the format and comparing extraction paths changed the interpretation. File and parser observations.

What ran on the Orange Pi RV2

The field device was an Orange Pi RV2 RISC-V board. Historical records identify a Ky X1 CPU; the October observer receipt reports eight logical CPUs, Linux 6.6.63-ky and Python 3.12.3. It successfully reran the small, offline trace and arithmetic observer against supplied data. It did not load the language model or recover a watermark key.

The practical tool chain was Orange Pi RV2 RISC-V/Ky X1 hardware, KY-ORT 1.2.2 / K1 for historical optimized ONNX Runtime import, a vendor Qwen2 INT4 0.5B ONNX-format model for the compact rows, AxisPL 0.1.4 qualification checks, and a Python keyless observer.

Earlier local-inference records also compare four compact-context runs with three broad-context runs. Mean time to first token was 12.45 versus 63.00 seconds. Those are useful resource observations, but the historical runs were not a randomized experiment. They do not prove a causal speedup or NPU acceleration. The native marked/unmarked pair described above was generated separately, on an ordinary x86-64 computer. Equipment, workloads and limits.

The strongest next question: can tokenization carry a keyed signal?

Tokenizers turn text into units a model can process. Changing those units seems like an attractive place to investigate a watermark: could a custom tokenizer, coupled to a key and a generation rule, leave a relationship that a matching detector can recognize in the finished text?

One simple version has already been ruled out in a toy control. All 24 ways of renaming a four-token vocabulary produced the same visible-text distributions when probabilities and decoding were renamed consistently. Giving the same choices new numbers changes the labels, not the language. Likewise, two token segmentations can decode to exactly the same bytes.

The broader question remains open. A key coupled to segmentation or generation choices might leave an observable relationship between the output and that key. The test must specify what operation changes the output, and how the detector uses the corresponding profile. An average distribution across keys can stay the same while a known key remains informative; a generic style classifier need not see the same relationship.

The next useful prototype would use owned generation and public test keys, with a detector fixed before testing. Correct-key, wrong-key, wrong-profile, clean-text and identity-only controls would distinguish an actual effect from relabeling. Held-out prompts, quality checks and declared editing tests would show whether the effect is useful. It also needs comparison with existing keyed sampling methods before anyone claims a new method family.

This proposal is untested. No current result establishes concealment from generic detectors, robustness after editing or superiority to provider methods. Its value is that it names a precise, testable next step. Proposal R22; negative control R21.

Other worthwhile tests concern ordinary separator positions, false marking and transplantation, mixed human/model passages, and calibration on held-out natural text. The research priorities explain the next observation that would distinguish each idea from its alternatives.

Attribution without false certainty

Defending provenance means resisting both concealed machine involvement and false implication. A purported anti-watermarking tool may claim success against a detector that never worked on its input. A purported detector may condemn an ordinary document because its threshold was tested on the wrong population. Both claims need controlled comparisons.

For editors, researchers and people protecting their own work, the practical procedure starts before the detector:

  1. Keep the original. Preserve original bytes, a hash, the acquisition route and an editing history. Retain the original privately when public release would expose personal material.
  2. Name the test. Record its model/profile, key access, input requirements, threshold and compatible positive control. An incompatible test does not become informative merely because it produces a number.
  3. Track the unit. Distinguish a generation from its copies, a sentence from its repeated occurrences, and the content from its container. Test extracted text and original files according to the evidence each can carry.
  4. Inspect ordinary alternatives. Compare parsers, template effects, quoting, repetition and editing before attributing an anomaly to a hidden system.
  5. State the narrow finding. A detected relationship can support involvement under a particular profile. It does not identify the user, measure human effort, settle ownership or verify the truth of a story.

Local, inspectable tooling helps preserve this chain. Sovereignty here means being able to keep and question the evidence, including a provider's claim and the local detector's own limitations. It does not mean assuming either one is infallible. The same discipline matters when provenance is used to assess deceptive media, although these text experiments do not measure image, audio or video watermark robustness.
